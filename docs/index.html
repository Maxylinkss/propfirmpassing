1. What a prop firm passing service is, and the three ways it gets delivered

A prop firm passing service is a paid arrangement in which someone other than the account holder completes a prop firm evaluation. You buy the attempt, not a strategy and not a signal feed. The deliverable is a challenge or verification phase that has reached its profit target while staying inside every rule the firm imposes — daily loss, maximum loss, drawdown type, minimum trading days and, at some firms, a consistency cap on how much of the profit any single day may contribute.

The economics are the reason the category exists. A typical evaluation costs the trader $500 at a $100k size, and the majority of attempts fail, so the real cost of getting funded on your own is the fee multiplied by the number of attempts it takes. That multiplier is invisible when you buy attempt one and becomes very visible when you are buying attempt four. A passing service replaces an unknown number of fee payments with one known payment, and it moves the failure risk onto the party doing the trading.

Not every business selling under this name works the same way, and the differences matter far more than the headline price. There are three delivery models in the market:

  • A trader trades your evaluation. A human runs your account against the firm's rules, reaches the target, and returns the login. This is the model most buyers assume they are buying, and it is the one every provider in the price table below is selling.
  • An algorithm trades it. An expert advisor or a script is attached to your account and passes the evaluation mechanically. Some of these are sold as software you install yourself, which is a different purchase entirely — you are buying a tool and keeping the risk, not buying the outcome.
  • The firm's own reset or deferral. The phrase "pay to pass" also gets used for a firm letting you defer its activation fee or settle the challenge fee out of your first payout. That is a payment plan from the firm, not a passing service, and nobody trades anything on your behalf.

Watch for a fourth thing that is not a service at all: an account rented or sold to you pre-passed, where you are handed credentials for someone else's funded account and a share of the payouts. That arrangement fails on the firm's own terms at the point of the payout, because the identity and the trading history do not belong to you. It is covered in the next section because it is frequently advertised as a passing service.

We pass your prop firm challenge — the precise version of that promise

This phrase is used loosely across the market, so here is exactly what it commits to. We pass your prop firm challenge means the evaluation's target is reached by us, against your firm's own published rulebook, with the failure risk carried by us for as long as the evaluation is running, and the account returned to you complete and documented. What it does not mean: the funded phase is not passed for you, and no payout is assured, because the funded account has its own drawdown and its own consistency condition. Anyone selling the phrase without that second sentence is selling the first half of an arrangement and keeping the part that matters to themselves.

Whatever the phrasing — pass prop firm challenge for me, passing prop firm challenge, we pass your prop firm challenge — the purchase is the same one: the attempt, the failure risk carried while it runs, and the account handed back complete. The terms check decides whether that purchase is worth making at your firm, it takes one message, and it comes first.

2. What it is not — four things being sold under the same name

Because "prop firm passing service" has no definition anybody enforces, a lot of unrelated products use it. A prop firm challenge passing service and a prop firm passing service are the same purchase described two ways, but a signal channel, a mirror account and a rented funded login all get sold under the phrase too. Four of them are worth separating out before you compare prices, because two are worthless to you and two carry risks you did not sign up for.

It is not a signal service. Signals tell you when to enter. They do nothing about the rule that actually ends most challenges, which is the drawdown. A trader with good signals and no risk framework breaches the daily loss limit in one bad session, and no amount of signal quality prevents that. If a provider's offer is a Telegram channel, you are not buying a pass.

It is not copy trading or a mirror account. Mirroring another trader's positions onto your evaluation account sounds equivalent to having them trade it, and in one respect it is worse: you still hold the account, you still own every rule breach, and the correlation between your account and theirs is a fact the firm can see. Where a firm prohibits third-party trading, copying is normally the exact behaviour the clause was written to catch.

It is not a rental. A pre-passed account sold or rented to you is not an evaluation pass. The firm's funded-account terms require the account holder to be the person trading, and the payout is where that gets checked — after you have paid, after you have traded, and usually after a profit has been made on an account that will not pay it out. The failure mode is not "you lose the $300"; it is losing the months of trading and the payout that was nearly yours.

It is not a guarantee of a funded payout. This distinction gets blurred in advertising, so it is worth stating precisely. A prop firm passing service ends at the evaluation. The funded account then has its own target, its own drawdown rules and, at many firms, a consistency requirement before the first withdrawal. Passing the evaluation does not make the payout automatic, and any provider implying otherwise is describing something they cannot control. What a pass does is convert a risk you carry repeatedly into a result you receive once.

The clean test. Ask what you receive at the end. A pass hands you an account that has met the firm's conditions. Anything else — a strategy document, a course, a channel invite, a login that belongs to somebody else — is a different product with the words "passing service" attached for search reasons.

3. The six steps from your first message to a funded account

The sequence is short and it is the same at every provider who works the way we do. What varies is the honesty of step one, which is why it is first.

Step 1 — The terms check, before any money moves

You name your firm and the exact plan you are trading. The clause that decides everything is the one governing who is permitted to trade the account — sometimes written as third-party or external account management, sometimes as "the account holder must be the only person placing trades", sometimes absent entirely, which is its own problem because your firm then decides case by case. We read that from the firm's own published document, quote the sentence, and answer yes or no.

Around one firm in five is a no. That answer is given before payment. A provider that takes the fee first has accepted your money on the single question it was supposed to answer, and you have no remedy if the answer turns out to be no.

Step 2 — Choosing the evaluation, not just the account size

The size determines the dollar figures and almost nothing else. What actually changes the assignment is phase count, drawdown type and minimum trading days. A 2-step challenge with a static 10% maximum loss is a different job from a 1-step evaluation with a 6% trailing threshold, and the trailing one is harder in a way a size comparison will never show you. This is the point where a firm's menu gets narrowed to the one plan worth buying.

Step 3 — The fee, flat, at any size

One payment for the evaluation, whatever the account size. The fee prices the work of passing a rulebook, and the work does not scale with the account. A $200k evaluation is not ten times the work of a $20k evaluation; the targets are the same percentages and the rules are the same rules. That is the whole argument for flat pricing, and it is why a percentage-of-target model ends up charging most for the accounts that are least difficult per dollar of target.

Step 4 — The pass, run to the firm's conditions

The account is traded against the plan the rulebook forces. Risk per position is set from the daily limit rather than from conviction, the target is approached in a sequence that respects any minimum-day rule, and where a consistency rule exists the profit is paced so no single session carries too much of the total. If the evaluation is failed while it is being run, the retry is ours, not another fee of yours.

Step 5 — Handover, with the account in a state that passes review

You get the account back with the target met and the rules intact, documented — what was reached, on which dates, against which version of the rulebook. The handover matters more than it sounds, because firms review the evaluation before funding it and an account whose numbers do not add up is a problem regardless of whether the target was reached.

Step 6 — The funded phase, which is yours

Passing the evaluation is the start of a new rulebook, not the end of the arrangement. The funded account has its own drawdown, usually a consistency condition before the first payout, and a payout schedule. Nothing about how the evaluation was passed changes any of it. Knowing the funded rules before you take the account is the difference between a pass that earns and a pass you cannot draw from.

5. The terms clause that decides whether a pass is worth anything

This is the section to read before anything else on this page, including our own price.

Every prop firm's terms define who may trade the account. The wording varies: some firms permit account management explicitly, some are silent, and some prohibit any person other than the account holder from placing trades. That clause, not the profit target, determines whether a prop firm passing service is a legitimate purchase for you or a way to spend $220 on something that gets voided at payout.

The failure mode is specific and worth understanding, because it does not happen at the evaluation. A firm that prohibits third-party trading will usually fund the account anyway — the evaluation review checks the numbers, not who was typing. The prohibition surfaces later, when you request a payout on a funded account, when the review looks at whether the account holder operated the account and the pattern of trading is inconsistent with the account holder's history. At that point the account can be closed and the profit withheld, and the evaluation fee, the passing fee and the trading time are all gone together.

What the check actually involves

  • The clause is read, not assumed. Not from a comparison article, not from a competitor's summary, and not from us. From your firm's own terms page or client agreement, on the date you are buying.
  • The plan matters as much as the firm. Firms running several products publish rules per plan, and a clause that permits management on one plan may not appear in another. Name the plan, not just the brand.
  • Silence is an answer, and not always a good one. A firm with no stated position is not a firm that permits the arrangement; it is a firm that reserves the decision. Treat an unstated position as a risk you are accepting knowingly.
  • The funded account's terms are a separate document. Evaluation terms and funded terms are published separately at every firm listed here. A clause that covers the evaluation says nothing about the payout stage.

The honest position. Where your firm prohibits third-party trading, we decline the work and it is not a sale we lose gracefully — paying for a pass that cannot survive the payout review is worse than not buying one. Where the clause permits it or the firm has no stated prohibition and you accept the risk, the terms check is documented and the pass proceeds.

6. The four rulebook families that decide a pass, and what they cost a plan

The target percentage is the number everyone quotes and the least interesting figure in the rulebook. Four other families decide whether a plan survives contact with the rules, and each one is recorded below with the date it was read and a link to the source.

Drawdown type

A daily limit resets and punishes a single bad session. A maximum loss limit is fixed and punishes the account as a whole. A trailing maximum loss punishes success: the floor rises as the account climbs, so a position that is comfortably above the target can be closed out by a retracement before the target is banked. Two firms with identical targets and identical percentages can be entirely different assignments depending on which of the three applies. A trailing threshold on unrealised profit is the hardest of them, because the account can be ended on a day that finishes net positive.

Minimum trading days

A target can be reached in three sessions. An evaluation that requires ten days cannot be finished in three. This single rule sets the honest timeline, which is why any quoted number of days means nothing without the firm named — the same target with a four-day minimum and a ten-day minimum are two different schedules, and only one of them can be delivered in under a week.

Consistency

Some firms cap the share of total profit any one day may contribute, often at 30% to 50%. This is the worst rule to breach, because it does not fail the evaluation — the target is reached and the account passes. It withholds the payout instead. A plan that reaches the target in three large sessions clears the target and fails the rule, which is the outcome with the least visible cause and the highest cost.

News and holding restrictions

Prohibitions on trading scheduled releases or holding through the weekend change when a position may be open rather than how large it may be. They matter most for plans that would otherwise run positions for days to accumulate profit slowly, and they are the rule most often discovered after a breach rather than before it.

7. How a challenge is actually traded, with the arithmetic

Nothing about a prop firm passing service is secret; the method is ordinary risk management applied to an unusual constraint. What makes it work is that the rules are arithmetic rather than judgement, so the plan can be derived from the rulebook before a single position is opened.

Take a standard 2-step forex evaluation at $100,000: a 10% phase-one target, a 5% daily loss limit and a 10% maximum loss. In dollars that is $10,000 to make, $5,000 you cannot lose in a day, and $10,000 you cannot lose at all.

The sizing comes from the daily limit, not from conviction

Risk 0.5% of the account — $500 — per position. A 2R winner is $1,000. To breach the daily limit at that size you would need ten losers in one session, which is the point: it is not a constraint the plan has to tiptoe around, it is a constraint the plan has already made irrelevant. The same target at 4% risk per position requires three losses in a day to end the account, and three losses happen.

Expectancy sets the pace

At 0.5% risk with a 60% win rate and an average 2R winner, expectancy is 0.6 × 2R − 0.4 × 1R = 0.8R, or $400 per trade. The $10,000 target is 25 trades at that expectancy. Spread across five sessions that is five trades a day, which is unremarkable for anyone trading an intraday method — and the whole plan is derived from three numbers in the rulebook plus one assumption about win rate.

Where the plan changes between phases

Phase two is usually half the target of phase one, so the pacing changes while the drawdown rules do not. The most common error at this stage is treating the smaller target as licence to increase size; the daily limit is unchanged, so the effect of raising risk in phase two is to move the account closer to the one rule that can still end it. A minimum-day rule cuts the other way, forcing the profit to be spread rather than concentrated, which is why a plan that passed phase one in three sessions often needs a different sequence to finish phase two.

What consistency does to the sequence

Where a consistency rule applies, the target is no longer the only finishing condition. If 40% of total profit must not come from one day, a $10,000 target caps any single session at $4,000, whether or not the account could have made more. The plan becomes a pacing exercise, and drifting into the session that makes the target also makes a payout problem. This is the rule that most often separates providers who reach a target from providers who deliver an account that pays.

8. Forex evaluations and futures evaluations are different jobs

Futures evaluations are sold by the same firms and are often assumed to be the same work at different instruments. They are not. Three things change, and each one alters the plan rather than the decoration.

Rules are denominated in dollars, not percentages

A futures firm publishes a $3,000 target and a $2,000 maximum loss on a $50,000 account rather than a percentage of anything. That means the difficulty changes with the contract you trade, not with the account size. The same $2,000 drawdown is 1,000 points at one micro Nasdaq contract and 40 points at one S&P mini. Two traders on identical rulebooks with identical skill can have completely different outcomes because of a sizing decision made at the start.

Contract quantisation replaces percentage sizing

You cannot trade 0.5 micro contracts. Position size moves in whole contracts, so the granularity of risk is set by the product: a micro is a fine control, a mini is a coarse one. On a $50,000 futures account, one mini S&P contract is a meaningful fraction of the daily limit as soon as its stop is a few points wide, and the plan has to be built around that rather than around a percentage that cannot be expressed.

Trailing thresholds respond to unrealised profit

Several futures firms trail the threshold on open profit, including intraday. A position showing $2,000 of unrealised gain has already raised the floor under the account, so a retracement can end the evaluation on a day that closes flat or positive. On a 2-step forex challenge with a static maximum loss, none of that is true. The practical difference is that futures rewards banking profit early and punishes letting a winner run to its target — the opposite of the habit that works on a static-drawdown forex evaluation.

What this means for buying a pass

A provider that treats them as interchangeable is quoting from a template. The questions that distinguish a real futures plan are narrow: which contracts, how the threshold trails on your firm's specific plan, and what the daily limit does when it is set at checkout rather than by the rulebook. If those get vague answers, the provider is pricing a forex challenge with a futures word in the title.

9. What can go wrong, stated without the padding

Every service in this category carries risks, and the ones that matter are not the risks most providers list. Here are the four that actually cost money.

The funded phase can still fail

A pass satisfies the evaluation. It does not satisfy the funded account, which has its own drawdown, its own target and usually a consistency requirement before the first withdrawal. A trader who receives a passed account and immediately trades it at three times the risk the evaluation plan used can end the funded account in a week, and no part of that is covered by what was bought. The evaluation is the beginning of the arrangement, and treating it as the finish line is the most expensive misreading of what a prop firm passing service does.

Terms can move after you have paid the firm

Small and newly launched firms revise their rulebooks often, and some revise them retroactively. An account passed under one version of a rulebook can be judged under another, which is a risk that sits with the firm's discretion. The mitigation is not a service, it is arithmetic: the longer an evaluation takes, the wider the window for the rules to move underneath it.

The payout is where the terms clause is tested

As covered above: a firm that prohibits third-party trading often funds the account anyway and withholds at the payout. Where the clause is unclear, the risk is real and it is disclosed here rather than discovered later. It is the single reason this page puts the terms check before the price.

Where a firm's terms prohibit the arrangement, the risk is not ours to manage on your behalf: buying a pass does not make it permitted, and no provider can make it permitted by asserting otherwise.

Retroactive fairness is not a thing

If a firm changes its drawdown from static to trailing while your evaluation is running, the account can fail under rules that did not exist when you bought the plan. Some firms honour the rulebook as it stood at purchase; some do not. This is disclosed here because it is the one risk a provider cannot price, and because finding it out after a failed pass is the version of this that costs you twice.

10. Passing it yourself or paying someone: the break-even

This is not a question of talent. It is a question of how many attempts you would take, and that number is more predictable than most traders believe.

Set the firm's fee at $500 per attempt at a $100k size and take a first-attempt pass rate of 20%. Your expected number of attempts is five, so the expected firm cost alone is $2,500 — and that is before counting the time each attempt takes, which at a four-week evaluation is roughly five months of calendar time. Run the same arithmetic at a 40% pass rate and it becomes $1,250 across about ten weeks. The number that decides the comparison is not the fee, it is the attempts multiplier.

Against that, a flat fee is a single, known quantity. At $220 the fee is under half the cost of one attempt at a $100k size, which is the honest way to compare them: not "$220 versus $2,500", but "$220 versus the first attempt you were going to buy anyway, with the retries no longer priced separately".

What you are actually buying

Three things, and it is worth being clear that none of them is an edge:

  • The rulebook read properly. The most common cause of a failed evaluation is a rule the trader did not read or read once and misremembered. That is a research cost, and it is the cheapest part of this.
  • The attempts you would have spent. At the sizes where firms charge most, this is the largest term in the arithmetic by a wide margin.
  • The time. A pass runs on the firm's minimum-day schedule rather than on the trader's, which usually means the calendar time is close to what you would spend anyway. On a trailing-drawdown futures evaluation, it is often shorter than a first attempt would have been.

What the comparison does not include

It does not include the value of learning the rulebook yourself, which is real and which a pass skips. It does not include the funded phase, which is governed by rules a pass does not satisfy. And it does not include the possibility that your own pass rate is much higher than the market average, in which case the arithmetic moves decisively in favour of doing it yourself — and this page would rather say that plainly than argue you out of it.

11. Who should not buy this

A service that refuses work is more useful than one that takes everything, so here is the version of this where the answer is no.

  • Your firm's terms prohibit third-party trading. The pass will be voided at the payout stage and the money is gone. This is not a marginal risk and it is not something to weigh against a low fee.
  • You want a funded account you then trade with the same plan. A pass hands you an account with a funded rulebook you have not practised. The risk profile changes at the moment of handover and the evaluation plan does not transfer.
  • The evaluation is not the constraint. If your blocker is that you have no method at all, a passed account is a passed account and then you are trading someone else's money with no framework. The evaluation is a test; it was never the problem.
  • You want the skill, not the account. Then the attempts are the tuition and they are worth paying for. Buy the challenge, fail it a few times, and read the rulebook yourself. This page is not for you and that is an honest answer rather than a lost sale.
  • The firm is new and revising terms monthly. Retroactive rule changes are a risk nobody can price, and the cheaper the firm, the more often its rulebook moves.

12. Seven checks before you pay any passing service

These apply to every provider in the table above, including us. They take about twenty minutes and they are the difference between buying a result and buying a story.

  1. Does the provider name the firms they will not work with? A list of refusals is evidence that a terms check happened. A page that says "all firms" without exception has not read anybody's terms.
  2. Is the price the price? Ask what happens at $200,000 and what happens after a failure. If either answer arrives as "it depends", get the specifics before paying — the retry term is the deciding number in section 4.
  3. Is a funded payout promised anywhere? It cannot be, because the funded phase is governed by rules the pass does not satisfy. A provider promising a payout has told you they will promise anything.
  4. What is the deliverable, in one sentence? A completed evaluation handed over documented. If the answer involves a strategy, a channel or a course, that is a different product.
  5. Where does the money sit if the pass fails? While the evaluation runs, a failure is the provider's cost, not a second fee of yours. Confirm that in writing.
  6. Can they explain your firm's drawdown type without looking it up? Trailing versus static is the first thing anyone competent will mention, and it will come up before price does.
  7. Do they tell you the funded rules before you take the account? The payout schedule and the consistency condition at your firm, stated up front, is the mark of someone who expects you to keep the account rather than just receive it.

Check them in this order, and stop at the first one that fails. A provider who fails check one or check three will pass the rest of the conversation comfortably, because those are the two that cannot be talked around.

The account is returned to you with the evaluation complete and documented, and the firm funds it under its own funded-account terms. Is a passing service the same as copy trading or signals?

No, and the differences are not cosmetic. Signals tell you when to enter and do nothing about the drawdown rule that ends most challenges. Copy trading leaves you holding the account and owning every rule breach, and where a firm prohibits third-party trading, mirroring positions is usually the exact behaviour the clause was written to catch. A passing service takes the attempt on and carries the failure risk while the evaluation runs.

Is it cheaper to pass the challenge myself?

It depends on how many attempts you would take. At a $500 firm fee and a 20% first-attempt pass rate, the expected cost of doing it yourself is five attempts, or about $2,500, plus roughly five months of calendar time. At a 40% rate it is about $1,250 across ten weeks. A flat $220 fee is under half the cost of a single attempt at a $100k size. If your own pass rate is high, the arithmetic moves decisively the other way and doing it yourself is cheaper.

Can you pass a futures evaluation like Apex or Topstep?

Yes, where the firm's terms allow it, and futures evaluations are a different job rather than the same job on different instruments. Their rules are denominated in dollars rather than percentages, which means difficulty changes with the contract you trade: a $2,000 drawdown is 1,000 points at one micro Nasdaq contract and 40 points at one S&P mini. Several futures firms also trail the threshold on open profit, so letting a winner run can end the account even on a day that closes positive.
